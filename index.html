Mountmellick

‘The soul’s the most precious thing,’ Ma always said. It’s a treasure inside you, packed with courage, that no shadow can steal. I’m Mary Millar, born on the sixth of July, 1831, in Mountmellick, Ireland. This is my story of facing fears with a memory chest, first imagined, then real. That chest carried Ma’s stories and my own courage, all the way across the sea.

Mountmellick wasn’t always a sad place. Before the blight came, people called it the “Manchester of Ireland” for its busy trades. Quakers — the Society of Friends — had settled there since the 1600s. Families like the Bewleys ran linen factories. The Beales milled flour and cotton. The Goodbodys and Pims made soap and bacon. They gave work to hundreds. The town hummed with about 4,800 souls in the streets, and more in the countryside round about.

School Days

When I was nine, in 1841, Mountmellick’s green fields and warm spuds filled my days. I pictured a memory box in my mind, holding joys like Ma’s tales of brave heroes.

School was a hedge school near a barn by the Quaker mill. Mr Doyle taught us reading, writing, and Irish words, such as ‘uisce’ for water. His Bible tales of David and Goliath fired my dreams of bravery.

Long ago, a young boy named David, who tended sheep, faced a huge, frightening giant named Goliath. Goliath made fun of David’s people. David, trusting in God, stood up to him with just a sling and a stone. With one shot, David hit Goliath’s forehead, knocked him down, and won the fight. It showed that even a kid with courage could beat a big bully. I wanted to be like David.

I wore a patched woollen dress, a pinafore, and a shawl, my feet bare to save shoes for church. Ma tied my hair with a ribbon, like one in her wooden box — a treasure I dreamed of adding to my own.

We played out in the field by the Owenass River, where the grass was soft and the air smelled of wildflowers, mixed with the clatter of the mills. My best mates, Katie, who was always taking charge, and Sean, full of mischief, were always with me. We joined hands and sang:

Ring a ring o’ roses,

A pocket full o’ posies,

Husha, husha!

We all tumble down!

At ‘tumble down,’ we flopped to the grass, me landing hard, Katie sprawling wide, and Sean rolling into the mud, cackling. The others kept laughing, but a queer chill went through me, like a cold wind in my chest. I didn’t know why. It felt as if hard times were coming, and I couldn’t shake the feeling. The Shadow Lord stirred, just for a moment, at the edge of the field. I picked up a small shining stone from the field and put it in my pocket, hoping it would keep me safe.

A dark fear crept in, though — a Shadow Lord from my nightmares. It came strongest the night Dad’s coughs stilled forever. I saw the Shadow Lord in my dream, a dark shape like a cloak of night, its eyes like cold stars. I cried for Ma, and she held me. Ma said my dream had warned of Dad’s spirit leaving for the stars. ‘The soft winds carried him to the stars, Mary,’ she said, ‘dancing with fairies.’ Her story chased my fear away. Her proverb about the soul became my secret gem.

I still had Katie and Sean, and I still had Ma. I didn’t know then of the hunger coming to scatter us.

Hungry Times

When I was fourteen, a terrible hunger, called the Great Famine, struck Ireland. Our spuds rotted black. The air smelled of rot, and Mountmellick turned quiet. By 1846 three-quarters of the harvest was lost in places. “Black ’47” was the hardest. Ireland’s people had grown thick as the grass — over eight million — all squeezed onto tiny plots. Landlords exported grain while we starved. It wasn’t just the blight. It was a system that crushed the poor.

In Mountmellick the population fell. Disease followed. The workhouse had been built for 800 people, but in 1847 it held 1,500. Yet the Quakers were our saviours. They set up soup kitchens, ladling out yellow porridge — rice, Indian meal and treacle. No strings attached. They fed about 210 children breakfast each week. Me and Ma queued up, shivering. Without them, more of us would have perished.

Ma worked ’til she was bone-tired, washing clothes, but we’d get only watery soup, tasting of nothing. She grew thin, and I feared the Shadow Lord might take her too. I’d cry, my heart aching for Dad’s laugh, but Ma’s lullabies sent me to sleep. Katie and Sean faded from my days, their families gone, and I missed their laughs. I never saw Katie or Sean again.

When I was fifteen, Ma sat me down, her eyes soft but tired. ‘Mary, love,’ she said, ‘we’ve a kind man, Thomas Harris, coming to live with us. He’ll be a new Dad, caring for us like your Dad in the stars would want.’ Soon I had two wee half-brothers, crying and laughing. Their giggles brought light, but feeding us all was harder, and Ma’s thin frame worried me. The Quakers gave us seeds for planting, though the ground stayed stubborn. They gave us blankets to keep us warm in the cold.

The Workhouse

In 1847, even the Quakers’ porridge wasn’t enough. Ma, her eyes full of tears, took me to the Mountmellick Workhouse, a grim place where poor folk toiled for scraps and a hard bed. As we stood at its heavy doors, Ma hugged me tight. I saw her thin face, and a cold feeling hit me — Ma wasn’t coming in with me. She left me there. She couldn’t carry us all any more.

The workhouse was grim, with too many folks squashed in. They gave me a scratchy dress that itched like nettles and cut my hair short to keep bugs away. I worked all day, washing and sewing, ’til my fingers were sore. At night they led us to the dormitory, a long, gloomy room stuffed with rows of narrow beds. Threadbare blankets barely warmed us. A single lantern flickered, and the air stank of mildew and unwashed bodies. I feared the Shadow Lord was hiding in the corners.

One night, alone in my bed, a chill ran through me. Then I saw it: the Shadow Lord, same as in my nightmare when Dad went, a dark shape like a cloak of night, its eyes like cold stars, gliding across the ward. I clutched my imagined treasure box, like David’s sling, and stood up. ‘Begone, Shadow Lord! Ye won’t have us!’ I called. My voice shook, but a wee girl nearby whimpered, scared as me. I crept to her, holding her hand, saying, ‘It’s grand. We’re all right.’ The shadow melted away, like mist at dawn, and morning light brought a Quaker woman with bread, her smile chasing the fear.

I thought of Ma often in that place. I never heard from Ma again.

The Chest and the Scheme

I was nearly three years in the workhouse, thinking that dreary dormitory would be my home forever, when I got great news. A Quaker lady, Sarah, in a plain bonnet, and a man, Lieutenant Henry, came with a plan called the Earl Grey Scheme. It was named after Henry Grey, the 3rd Earl Grey, who was Secretary of State for the Colonies. Between 1848 and 1850, over four thousand girls were sent from the workhouses to Australia to work as maids, and to ease the crowding at home. Sarah saw my tough hands and hoping eyes, saying, ‘You’re strong, Mary, like the linen we weave here.’

Sarah led me to a room where a green wooden chest waited, its iron clasp gleaming. It was a proper travel box, heavy, and full of what I might need. Inside were two cotton dresses (blue and brown), a white apron, two pairs of stockings, black shoes, a Bible, a needle and thread in a tin, a wooden comb, and soap in cloth. A canvas bag marked ‘Mary Millar’ held extras. ‘This is yours, Mary,’ Sarah said. ‘Carry your future in it.’

I put Ma’s story in it, and Dad’s laugh, and the Quakers’ kindness, and my smooth stone. I vowed to add my own treasures too. I was eighteen.

Plymouth

From Mountmellick we were taken to Plymouth, in England, and we waited at the immigration depot while the ship was made ready. The officers looked us over once more. I’d never seen the sea. I sat on my chest in a cold shed, smelling salt for the first time, and thought of Ma’s proverb. The Shadow Lord was there in my thoughts, but I held the chest and waited.

Voyage on the Tippoo Saib

The Tippoo Saib left Plymouth on the eighth of April, 1850, packed with 297 of us girls, some families, and a crew. Dr Church was the surgeon, and his wife was with him. Below decks the air was stuffy, like a cupboard of old socks, and the ship’s rocking made our tummies wobble.

My friend Ellie, from Galway, got so sick she’d just sit and hug a bucket, her face pale as a cloud. I’d hold her hand, humming Ma’s lullaby: ‘Hush, love, the storm will pass.’ Bridget, from Mayo, was quiet as a mouse, her eyes often down. We three stayed close. I met Margaret, who taught me a few Mountmellick stitches — flowers sewn pretty, a Quaker craft. I worked on a cloth scrap, wanting it for my treasure box.

A week out from Plymouth, a pulley high on the mast squealed like a ghost. Tom, a cadet sailor with a mop of brown hair and a cheeky grin, climbed up to grease it. His foot slipped, and he dangled from the rope, legs kicking! ‘Hold on!’ Ellie gasped. A burly sailor, the first mate, hauled him to safety. Tom’s cap flew off into the sea. ‘Nearly joined the fishes!’ he panted, pale but grinning. To thank us for cheering, Tom tied a small rope knot, shaped like a star, and handed it to me. ‘For your box, Mary, to keep the Shadow Lord quiet,’ he said, all mischief and no sweet talk. I tucked the knot in my chest.

Late one April night, a great storm screamed in, with waves taller than the deck. The ship groaned, and a crate fell. I heard wee Ellie crying, trapped underneath. I crawled to her, the floor tilting, and pushed ’til the crate budged. Her arms clung to me. In the lightning I saw it — the Shadow Lord, same as at the workhouse, a dark shape like a cloak of night, eyes like cold stars, rising from the waves. I stood and held my treasure box high. ‘Ye’ll not have us, Lord of the Shadows!’ I shouted. It shrank back into the mist. The next morning the storm had died. Dolphins broke through the sea, leaping, and we all cheered. Days later, a whale came close, its eye gleaming, spouting water like a fountain. I gripped my box and felt braver.

Tenerife and Cape Town

In early May we reached Santa Cruz in Tenerife. Dr Church let me, Ellie, Margaret and Bridget walk ashore with a crewman. The docks were warm, smelling of salt and sweet fruit. At a stall piled with oranges, a vendor in a straw hat grinned and handed us some. The first bite was juicy and sweet — fruit we’d hardly tasted in the hungry years. Ellie squealed, ‘It’s so sweet!’ Her chin was dripping. Even quiet Bridget smiled. I tucked a curly peel in my chest. Back on the ship we shared the extra orange with girls who’d been sick or sad. One of them was Kate, who missed her brother. Their smiles lit the hold.

In mid-June we reached Cape Town, where Table Mountain stood like a giant’s table, wrapped in a cloudy cloth that made Ellie giggle. The docks were cooler and windy. A tall lady with a yellow headscarf stood by a cart of red apples. ‘For you, young ones,’ she said, and gave us each an apple, and an extra for the ship. As we munched, Tom ran past, chasing a gang of small grey monkeys! One had snatched his new cap. ‘Oi, you little thieves!’ he shouted, laughing. ‘Help me, ladies, or I’m cap-less again!’ I tossed him an apple slice, yelling, ‘Bribe ’em!’ A monkey dropped the cap to grab the treat. Tom scooped it up, bowed, and tossed us a shiny penny. Bridget tucked it in her pocket, saying, ‘For luck.’ We shared the extra apple below decks, and the hold buzzed with giggles.

One night Bridget told me and Ellie about Mayo. She grew up in a stone cottage in Ballina. When she was fourteen the hunger came. She lost her Ma and her brother, then her Dad, and went to the Ballina Workhouse. They picked her for the Earl Grey Scheme. ‘They said Australia’s a chance,’ she told us. She packed a pebble from Mayo’s shore. ‘You’re my new sisters,’ she said, and we hugged her.

Ellie told us about Galway, a windy city by the sea. She lived near the Claddagh, where fishing boats bobbed. Her Ma was a seamstress, and her Dad fished with her big brother, Sean. Then the hunger hit, and her Ma and Dad were gone. She went to the Galway Workhouse. Sean got a job on a ship and left, promising to find her later. She packed a charcoal stub and a gull feather, hoping to sketch her new home. ‘You’re my family now,’ she said, and we squeezed her tight.

In July I turned nineteen on the ship. The sea went on and on, and my chest went with me.

Sydney

On the twenty-ninth and thirtieth of July, 1850, the Tippoo Saib came into Sydney Harbour. The water sparkled, and green hills hugged the shore. We had to stay on the ship about a week before we were allowed ashore. Then we were taken to Hyde Park Barracks.

Our bags were heavy, and Bridget, tired, struggled with hers. Tom appeared, his cap dusty but on. ‘Need a hand, ladies?’ he asked, grinning. ‘These bags are heavier than monkeys!’ he teased, hoisting Bridget’s bag onto his shoulder. At the barracks gate he tipped his cap and slipped us a shiny pebble from the dock. ‘For luck!’ he said. I tucked mine in my treasure box.

I never saw Tom again after that day at the gate.

Inside the barracks we got a hot meal — bread, mutton and peas, the best since Tenerife’s orange. Ellie chattered about being a cook. Bridget, nibbling bread, said, ‘I want to work with animals.’ I dreamed of sewing, maybe with my new stitches. That afternoon we walked in a courtyard. A bird with a rainbow tail flitted by. Ellie gasped, ‘It’s a flying jewel!’ We linked arms, three friends ready for Australia.

The Eagle

I was at the barracks through August. On the second of September, 1850, I was one of fifteen girls sent north to Moreton Bay, where work was needed. They picked me because I could sew, thanks to Margaret’s lessons. Ellie and Bridget hugged me tight, promising to write. I packed my treasure box, adding a tiny barracks button as a memory, and boarded the steamer Eagle.

The Eagle was smaller than the Tippoo Saib, with a smokestack and a deck that hummed under my feet. It took a few days, hugging the shore, past sandy beaches and rocky cliffs. The air smelled of salt and coal. I stood by the rail, clutching my box, watching seagulls swoop. The Shadow Lord tried to whisper about the unknown, but I thought of Tom’s knot and my friends’ hugs, and I felt ready.

When we reached Moreton Bay, the Brisbane River sparkled, winding through green hills. I looked out at Queen’s Wharf. In 1850 Moreton Bay was a frontier district of New South Wales, still getting over the old convict days, which had ended about eight years before. Brisbane Town had wooden buildings, a few stone ones, and dusty tracks instead of proper roads. Aboriginal peoples had lived there for tens of thousands of years, long before any of our ships arrived.

Bellevue

From Brisbane I was sent on into the Brisbane Valley. The road took me past Ipswich and on toward Wivenhoe, through wide grass and gum trees, with sheep on the plains and strange birds calling. I went to Bellevue, a sheep station near Wivenhoe. Mrs Carter, who kept the house, welcomed me. ‘You’ll be our junior maid, Mary,’ she said, showing me to my own small room with a cot and a window facing the plains.

My first job was to mend a pile of shirts and curtains. I sat at a wooden table, my treasure box beside me, and stitched as neatly as I could. Mrs Carter smiled, saying, ‘You’ve a fine hand, Mary!’ The work was hard — sweeping floors, washing dishes, and carrying water — but the station was alive with sounds: sheep baaing, workers shouting, and a funny bird that laughed like Tom’s cheeky grin. At supper I ate damper bread and mutton stew with the Carters, warm and filling. A worker played a fiddle, and I tapped my foot, feeling like part of a new family.

That night, lying on my cot, I looked out at the starry sky, so big it made my heart race. I added a tiny gum leaf to my treasure box, a piece of this new land.

Letters from Friends

Then, in October, letters found me at Bellevue. I kept them in my chest.

Sydney, New South Wales

15 October 1850

Dear Mary,

I’ve been itching to write you since we parted at Hyde Park Barracks! My heart’s still full of our Tippoo Saib days — those wild storms, Tenerife’s oranges, and Tom’s monkey chase in Cape Town. I miss your brave spirit and that green treasure chest of yours. I hope it’s keeping the Shadow Lord far from your new home.

After you left for the Eagle, I stayed at the barracks a bit longer. I’d sneak my charcoal stub from Galway to draw those rainbow-tailed birds on scrap paper. Bridget stayed too, quiet as ever, but she smiled more, talking of working with horses.

A week after you sailed, a kind couple, the O’Connors, picked me for a job in Parramatta, a town just west of Sydney, about a day’s ride by cart. They run a boarding house, a big wooden place with a verandah where I sweep and serve tea. Mrs O’Connor is stern but fair, and she loves my neat stitches — thanks to Margaret’s lessons on the ship. Mr O’Connor’s got a laugh like my Dad, warm and loud, and he lets me sketch the guests when chores are done.

I’ve a wee room with a window facing a gum tree. I’ve drawn it ten times already. The work’s hard, and my hands ache, but I’m saving pennies for paints. Sometimes I hum Ma’s lullabies from Galway, the ones I sang when you held my hand in that storm. I’ve tucked a gum leaf in my sketchbook, like you’d put in your chest.

I saw a kangaroo hop past last week, Mary! It made me laugh, thinking of you and Bridget clapping at Tom’s antics. I miss our trio. Write me and tell me of Bellevue. Do they have kangaroos there? Are you still stitching those flowers? I dream of us meeting again, maybe in Sydney’s markets, sharing an orange like old times. Keep that chest close. Your Galway pal is cheering you on, brave as David with his sling.

Yours forever,
Ellie

P.S. I’ve drawn the birds on the back — imagine them flying to your treasure chest!

Richmond, New South Wales

20 October 1850

Dear Mary,

It’s me, Bridget, writing with shaky hands from a place called Richmond. I’ve been meaning to send you word since we left Hyde Park Barracks, but it’s taken me time to find my courage, like you always had with that green treasure chest of yours. I miss our quiet talks on the Tippoo Saib — the storm when you saved Ellie, the oranges in Tenerife, and Tom’s funny cap chase. I hope your chest is full of new treasures up in Moreton Bay, keeping the Shadow Lord far away.

After you sailed on the Eagle, Ellie and I stayed at the barracks, sewing and scrubbing. A week later, a farmer, Mr Walsh, and his wife chose me for their farm in Richmond, a quiet spot north-west of Sydney, about a day’s ride. They needed a girl to help with the house and animals, and I smiled when they asked, thinking of Finn, my dog back home.

I live in a small room off the kitchen, with a window overlooking sheep and a paddock where I feed the horses. Mrs Walsh is kind, showing me how to milk cows, and Mr Walsh lets me brush the horses. My hands are sore from chores, but I love the animals. I’ve tucked a horsehair from the grey mare into my apron pocket, like you’d add to your chest. At night I hear crickets, not the workhouse coughs.

I saw a kangaroo once, hopping near the creek, and it made me think of you and Ellie laughing. I miss your stitches and Ellie’s chatter, but I’m finding my way. Mrs Walsh says I’ve a gentle touch with the lambs, and I hope Ma and Dad would be proud.

Please write and tell me of Bellevue Station. Keep that chest close, and know I’m thinking of you, brave as David. I’ve pressed a gum leaf from the creek in here, a little gift for your chest. Maybe one day we’ll meet again.

Your friend always,
Bridget

A Happy Home

I had come all the way from Ireland. Like David facing Goliath, I beat the dark shadows I fancied round me, using my treasure box and Ma’s soft winds tale as my sling. My box became real, and it stayed with me, holding my dresses, stitches, and dreams. It reminded me of Ma, and the Quakers’ kindness, and the friends who crossed the sea with me. In quieter moments I thought of the Aboriginal people whose Country this had been long before any ship arrived.

The Shadow Lord was quiet. Now I have new friends, Bellevue’s fields all round, and I have a happy home. I am Mary Millar, and my Earl Grey chest holds the story of my adventure. Just like Ma’s proverb, my soul has been my truest treasure, carrying me through shadows and seas, from Mountmellick’s fields to Bellevue’s wide plains.

— End —

Author’s Note

This story is historical fiction based on the real Mary Millar — also recorded as Mary Miller — an Earl Grey girl from the Mountmellick Workhouse, and an ancestor in our family. It is not a composite character. Some scenes, conversations and friends are imagined, so a young reader can walk with her. I have not invented facts where the record can be checked, and I have left some things uncertain rather than fill them in.

On the record: Mary was born on 6 July 1831. She left Plymouth on the Tippoo Saib on 8 April 1850, with about 297 orphan girls. Dr Church was the surgeon superintendent. The ship stopped at Tenerife and Cape Town, and came into Sydney on 29–30 July 1850 (the official arrival date is 30 July). The girls stayed on board about a week, then went to Hyde Park Barracks. On 2 September 1850 Mary was one of fifteen girls sent to Moreton Bay on the steamer Eagle. By 1851–52 she was connected with Bellevue, a sheep station in the Brisbane Valley, near Wivenhoe. It is not documented who first employed her in 1850. She married Richard Browning on 27 October 1851. She died in Ipswich in 1906.

The Earl Grey Scheme ran from 1848 to 1850 and was named after Henry Grey, the 3rd Earl Grey, Secretary of State for the Colonies. More than four thousand young Irish women and girls were sent from workhouses to Australia as domestic servants. Lieutenant Henry took part in the selection. The Great Famine was made far worse by poverty and government policies. The Quakers of Mountmellick provided vital relief. Moreton Bay in 1850 was a frontier district of New South Wales. Aboriginal peoples had lived there for tens of thousands of years.

Imagined in this telling: Ellie, Bridget, Tom, Margaret, Kate, Mr Doyle, Katie, Sean, Sarah the Quaker, and Mrs Carter; the O’Connors and the Walshes in the letters; the Shadow Lord; the treasures added to the chest; conversations, the exact first days at Bellevue, and the letters themselves. The shipping list recorded that Mary could not read or write, and she signed her marriage certificate with a mark, so the hedge school and the letters are imagined too. What became of her mother is not certain. In this story Mary never heard from her again.

Many people have heard that Ring a Ring o’ Roses is about the plague, but the song wasn’t printed until the 1880s, and historians don’t accept that link. Mary’s chill as the children fall down is her own feeling of what was coming, not the meaning of the song.

The Shadow Lord and the memory chest help a young person name fear and hold on to courage.
