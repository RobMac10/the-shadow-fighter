Mountmellick Before the Shadows

Ma always said the soul is the most precious thing. It is a glowing treasure inside you, packed with courage that no shadow can steal. I am Mary Millar, born in Mountmellick, Ireland. This is the story of how I faced my fears with a memory chest — first imagined, then real — and how that chest carried me across the sea to a new country.

Mountmellick was not always a place of sorrow. Before the blight came, people called it the “Manchester of Ireland” for its busy trades. Quakers — the Society of Friends — had settled there since the 1600s. Families like the Bewleys ran linen factories that employed over a hundred and fifty people. The Beales milled flour and cotton. The Goodbodys and Pims made soap and bacon. They gave work to hundreds. The town hummed with about 4,800 souls in the streets and more in the countryside.

When I was seven, in 1841, the green fields and warm potatoes filled my days with sparkle. I pictured a little box in my mind, holding joys like Ma’s tales of brave heroes. A dark fear crept in — a Shadow Lord from my nightmares. It came strongest the night Dad’s coughs finally stilled forever. Ma said my dream had warned that his spirit was leaving for the stars. She told me the soft winds carried him, dancing with the fairies. That story became my secret gem.

School Days and the First Shadow

School was a hedge school near the Quaker mill. Mr Doyle taught us reading, writing and Irish words. His tale of David and Goliath fired my dreams. A young shepherd boy faced a giant with only a sling and a stone, and won. I wanted to be like David.

We played by the Owenass River with Katie and Sean, singing Ring a Ring o’ Roses. Mr Doyle told us the old plague tale behind the song. That night the Shadow Lord felt closer. I picked up a smooth stone and kept it in my pocket for courage.

Hungry Times

When I was eleven the Great Famine struck. A strange rot turned the potatoes to stinking mush. By 1846 three-quarters of the harvest was lost in places. “Black ’47” was the hardest. Ireland’s people had grown thick as the grass — over eight million — all squeezed onto tiny plots. Landlords exported grain while we starved. It was not just the blight. It was a system that crushed the poor.

In Mountmellick the population plummeted. Disease followed. The workhouse swelled far beyond its numbers. Yet the Quakers set up soup kitchens with rice, Indian meal and treacle. No strings attached. They fed about 210 children breakfast each week. Without them, more of us would have perished.

Ma worked until she was bone-tired. She grew thin. I feared the Shadow Lord might take her too. By thirteen she sat me down. “Mary, love,” she said, “we’ve a kind man, Thomas Harris, coming to live with us.” Soon I had two wee half-brothers.

The Workhouse

In 1848, even the Quakers’ porridge was not enough. Ma took me to the Mountmellick Workhouse. As we stood at its heavy doors she hugged me tight. A cold truth hit me — Ma was not coming back. She left me behind so she could take the little ones to Canada.

The workhouse was grim. They gave me a scratchy dress and cut my hair short. I worked all day. At night the dormitory was a long, gloomy room. One night I saw the Shadow Lord. I clutched my imagined treasure box and called out, “Begone!” The fear melted. Morning light brought a Quaker woman with bread.

The Chest and the Scheme

Years later Sarah the Quaker and Lieutenant Henry came with the Earl Grey Scheme. Over four thousand girls were sent to Australia as domestic servants between 1848 and 1850. Sarah gave me a real green wooden chest filled with dresses, a Bible, needle and thread, and soap. “Carry your future in it,” she said. I was eighteen when I boarded the Tippoo Saib.

Voyage on the Tippoo Saib

The Tippoo Saib left Plymouth with 297 of us girls. A great storm nearly took us. I freed Ellie from under a crate and faced the Shadow Lord with my chest held high. Tom the sailor nearly fell from a pulley and gave me a star-shaped rope knot. In Tenerife we tasted oranges like sunshine. In Cape Town monkeys stole Tom’s cap. We became sisters.

Sydney

On 29 July 1850 we arrived in Sydney Harbour. Tom helped with our bags. We stayed at Hyde Park Barracks. After three weeks I was chosen for Moreton Bay because I could sew.

North to Moreton Bay and Bellevue

I travelled on the steamer Eagle. In 1850 Moreton Bay was a frontier district. Aboriginal peoples had lived there for tens of thousands of years. From Ipswich I was hired as a maid at Bellevue Station on the Darling Downs. Mrs Carter welcomed me. I mended curtains and added a gum leaf to my chest. The Shadow Lord was quiet.

A Happy Home

I had come all the way from Ireland. Like David facing Goliath, I had used courage and memory as my sling. The box became real and stayed with me. In quieter moments I thought of the Aboriginal people whose Country this had been long before any ship arrived. For now the chest was full, and a new day was beginning under the wide Australian sky.

Author’s Note

This story is historical fiction based on the real experiences of the Earl Grey girls — more than four thousand young Irish women and girls sent from workhouses to Australia between 1848 and 1850. Mary Millar is a composite character. The cover is original artwork. The full-page images are public-domain primary sources from the period. The Great Famine was made far worse by poverty and government policies. The Quakers of Mountmellick provided vital relief. Moreton Bay in 1850 was a frontier district. Aboriginal peoples had lived there for tens of thousands of years. The Shadow Lord and memory chest help a young person name fear and hold on to courage.

Dedicated especially to Phoebe Adele McKean, avid reader and creative writer, and to all the grandchildren.
